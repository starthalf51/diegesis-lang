In the cloister the light lay pale and even across the flagstones, and the pillars threw their shadows long toward the inner court. Ulysses stood with his son near the armoury door, and he spoke low so that the sound would not carry.

"Telemachus, we must get the armour together and take it down inside."

Euryclea came in from the hall, her hands wrapped in her apron, and Telemachus turned to her at once.

"Nurse, shut the women up in their room, while I take the armour that my father left behind him down into the store room."

She stood still a moment, her old eyes moving from the young man's face to the far doorway. "But who is to go with you and light you to the store-room?" she asked.

She did not wait for an answer. She turned and went out again, and the cloister was quiet.

Minerva came through the arch then, in the shape of a man, and stood where the light fell across her. Telemachus saw her and went to her side, and then he turned to the ragged old man by the pillar and spoke to him as to Ulysses, his father, and his voice shook a little.

"Father, my eyes behold a great marvel … Surely there is some god here."

"Hush, hold your peace and ask no questions … Get you to your bed."

The goddess moved away, and Telemachus went after her, and the two of them passed out of the cloister together. Ulysses remained where he was, leaning on his staff, with his rags drawn about him.

Penelope entered slowly, veiled, with her women about her. She stopped before the old man and looked at him for a long while before she spoke.

"Stranger, I shall first ask you who and whence are you?"

Ulysses answered her with a fluent tale, shaping the words as a man does who has told them many times. "There is a fair and fruitful island in mid-ocean called Crete … I, who am the younger, am called Aethon."

Penelope listened without any sign of belief or disbelief in her face. When he had done she said nothing of it, but he went on, and his voice grew warmer as he told her that Ulysses was alive and coming home to Ithaca. "I have lately heard of Ulysses as being alive and on his way home … Ulysses will return in this self same year."

"May it be even so," Penelope said, "but I know very well how it will be. Ulysses will not return."

Euryclea came back into the cloister with a basin in her arms, and Penelope turned to her.

"Euryclea, and wash your master's age-mate."

The old woman set down the basin and knelt by the stranger's feet. She looked up at him, and then at her mistress, and she spoke as one who has seen a thing she cannot quite credit. "No one ever yet came who was so like Ulysses in figure, voice, and feet as you are."

"Those who have seen us both have always said we were wonderfully like each other," Ulysses replied.

Minerva came again into the cloister, unseen by most of them, and she turned Penelope's thoughts elsewhere. Penelope was unable to look in that direction and observe what was going on, for Minerva had diverted her attention. The goddess went out again, and the arch was empty.

Euryclea had taken the old man's foot in both hands to wash it. As soon as she had got the scarred limb in her hands and had well hold of it, she recognised it. She let the leg fall and stared up at him, and her lips moved.

"My dear child, I am sure you must be Ulysses himself, only I did not know you till I had actually touched and handled you."

She bent close, and Ulysses leaned down to her and spoke into her ear, so softly that only she could hear.

"Nurse, do you wish to be the ruin of me … now that after twenty years of wandering I am at last come to my own home again? Since it has been borne in upon you by heaven to recognise me, hold your tongue."

"I will hold my tongue like a stone or a piece of iron," Euryclea whispered back.

Ulysses drew his seat nearer to the fire to warm himself, and hid the scar under his rags. The old woman rose slowly and went on with her washing, and the basin steamed between them.

Penelope turned back toward the stranger. "Stranger, I should like to speak with you briefly about another matter."

Euryclea went out with her basin, Ulysses rose and followed his queen, and Penelope went out after them, and the cloister stood empty in the pale light.
