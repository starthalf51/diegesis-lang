A guest is coming, Chou Yü told the officers of Wu who stood about his tent, and they straightened and made way for the visitor. Chiang Kan entered with his head lowered and his robe brushing the mats, and he looked about the tent with the careful, friendly air of a man who has come a long way to see an old acquaintance.

"You have been well, I hope, since last we met," he said.

Chou Yü rose and took his hand. He looked at the traveller's dusty sleeves and worn boots, and his voice grew warm with sympathy. "You have wandered far and suffered much in this task of emissary in the Tsʻao cause. Sit, sit. You must be exhausted."

Chiang Kan laughed and shook his head. "I have not seen you for a very long time, and I came to visit you for the sake of old times. Why do you call me an emissary for the Tsʻao cause?"

"Though I am not so clever a musician as Shih Kuang," Chou Yü answered, smiling, "yet I can comprehend the thought behind the music."

He turned to the officers, who had gathered near the entrance. "He is an old fellow student of mine, and we are pledged friends."

Then he called T'aishih Tzŭ forward and handed him a token of office. "You take this and wear it for the day as master of the feast. This day we meet only as friends, and if any one shall begin a discussion of the questions at issue between Tsʻao Tsʻao and our country, just slay him."

T'aishih Tzŭ bowed and fastened the token to his sash. Chou Yü then led Chiang Kan to the edge of the camp, where the soldiers were drilling in the dust, and he spread his arm toward them with evident pride.

"Do you not think my soldiers a fine lot of fellows?"

Chiang Kan watched the ranks wheel and stamp, and he nodded slowly. Chou Yü glanced sideways at him, and then asked again, more quietly, "Is it possible to move such as I am?"

The officers of Wu drew T'aishih Tzŭ away at once, and the group withdrew from the drill ground and back toward the tents, leaving the two men alone for a moment. Chou Yü then returned to his tent with Chiang Kan following, and he sat down heavily among his cushions. He began to drink, and before long his speech grew thick and his eyes unfocused. He let his head loll against his arm and muttered, laughing at nothing, until his guest was sure the wine had taken him entirely.

While Chou Yü seemed to sleep, Chiang Kan rose quietly and looked over the papers scattered on a stand near the couch. Among them lay a letter marked as from Chang Yün and Tsʻai Mao. He unfolded it and read it closely, his lips moving slightly. When he had finished, he sat back on his heels, and a thought came to him: "Those two, he decided, were secretly in the service of Wu all along." Then he folded the letter and slipped it into the breast of his robe.

Chou Yü stirred, opened his eyes, and smiled at him drowsily. "Friend, I am going to let you see Tsʻao's head in a day or two."

Before Chiang Kan could answer, the officers of Wu came hurrying in, and one of them bent to whisper in Chou Yü's ear: "A man has arrived from the north."

Chou Yü got up at once, steady now, and went out with the officer, leaving Chiang Kan standing in the tent. The other officers pressed in and turned toward him with grave faces. "Chang and Tsʻai, the two commanders, have come," one of them announced loudly, and then they all went out again.

Chiang Kan waited until the voices had faded. Then he went quickly to the entrance, glanced left and right, and slipped out. He did not look back. He walked fast past the picket lines, and then broke into a run.

Chou Yü came back into the tent alone, looked at the empty space by the couch, and then went out again, and the tent was left to the wind.
