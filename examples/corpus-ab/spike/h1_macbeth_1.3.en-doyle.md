The heath lay grey under a low sky, and the wind moved through the broom and the bracken without any sound of its own. Far off, where the land fell away toward the firth, something moved in the mist, and then the voices came up out of it, thin and close together.

"The weird sisters, hand in hand,
Posters of the sea and land,"

They were three women, or three shapes that had taken the look of women, bent and bearded at the chin, their skins the colour of old parchment. They did not come toward the two men so much as wait for them, as a thing waits that has known for a long time what will arrive.

Macbeth had ridden out from Forres that morning with Banquo at his side, and they had talked of little until the road gave out upon the open moor. He looked now at the sky, which had been dark with rain and was already clearing at its edges, and he found that his mouth had formed the words before he had decided to speak them.

"So foul and fair a day I have not seen."

He heard the words go out over the wet grass and did not know whether he had meant them as praise or warning. Banquo said nothing. His horse shifted under him, and Macbeth felt the cold of the morning settle into his hands on the reins.

He dismounted, or stood where he was with the reins slack, and looked at the three women, and he could not afterward remember which of them moved first. He said, more sharply than he intended, "Speak, if you can: what are you?"

The first of them lifted her face. Her eyes were pale and wet and fixed on him, and she spoke as if she had been waiting a long time to say it.

"All hail, Macbeth! hail to thee, thane of Glamis!"

It was his own name, and his own title, given back to him from a stranger's mouth, and the title was true. He had held Glamis since his father died. Yet he had never heard it spoken like that, as if it were a thing already finished and only waiting to be noticed by the world. He felt the blood come up into his face and then go out of it.

The second woman spoke before he could answer.

"All hail, Macbeth! hail to thee, thane of Cawdor!"

He opened his mouth and closed it again. Cawdor was not his. It was another man's, and that man was living, or had been living when Macbeth last heard of him. The words had no place in the day he had planned, and yet they sat in his mind as easily as the first title had.

The third sister leaned toward him, and her voice dropped to something low and almost tender.

"All hail, Macbeth, that shalt be king hereafter!"

Something in his chest turned over. He did not look at Banquo, though he could feel him there, watching, and he understood that he was afraid of what his face would show.

Then the third sister turned from him to his companion, and Macbeth heard her say, in the same low voice, "Thou shalt get kings, though thou be none." Banquo laughed, short and uneasy, and said nothing to her. Macbeth heard the words and set them beside the others. Banquo's sons, then, or his sons' sons, would sit where Macbeth now hoped to sit. He told himself this carefully and then told himself again, as though repetition could make it less strange, that Banquo's descendants would be kings.

He said aloud, to the three of them, "By Sinel's death I know I am thane of Glamis." His father's death had been plain enough, and the title had come with it, but he had not thought until now that the thing could be known by anyone else.

"But how of Cawdor?" he said. "The thane of Cawdor lives: a prosperous gentleman; and to be king / Stands not within the prospect of belief." He had meant to say it lightly, and it came out rough. "The thane of Cawdor lives," he said again, and then, to make it firmer, "A prosperous gentleman."

The three women did not answer him. The mist behind them thickened and they were not standing where they had stood; there was only the grey heath and the broom moving in the wind. Macbeth stared at the place where they had been and felt, for the first time, how cold his hands were.

He turned to Banquo, who had come up beside him and was looking at the empty moor with an expression Macbeth could not read. "Your children shall be kings," Macbeth said, and he heard himself say it with a kind of defiance, as though Banquo had asked for it.

Banquo shook his head slowly. "You shall be king," he said, and Macbeth understood then that Banquo was speaking of the thing as if it were already settled, that he too had heard the sisters and believed what they had said.

Before Macbeth could answer, two riders came over the rise. Angus rode first, and Ross behind him, their cloaks dark with the damp. Ross dismounted quickly and came toward Macbeth with his hat in his hand.

"Thane," Ross said. "The king has sent me to thank you. He bade me, from him, call thee thane of Cawdor."

Macbeth looked at him. The words were the same as the sisters', and they were said by a man who had no reason to flatter him. He felt the ground under his feet become less certain.

"The thane of Cawdor lives," Macbeth said, and his voice was unsteady. "Why do you dress me / In borrow'd robes?"

Angus stepped forward. He was grave and his face was tired. "Who was the thane lives yet; / But under heavy judgment bears that life / Which he deserves to lose," he said, and paused, as if weighing whether to go on. Then he went on. "Treasons capital, confess'd and proved, / Have overthrown him."

Macbeth heard this and did not move. Cawdor was condemned. He was still alive, and under judgment, and his life would be taken from him for what he had done. Macbeth looked at Angus's face and found no mercy in it, only the plain fact of a sentence already given.

Under his breath, almost without sound, Macbeth said, "Glamis," as if testing the word against the wind. Then, lower still, "and thane of Cawdor!" He stopped there, and his hand tightened on the reins, and he thought, with a clarity that frightened him, *The greatest is behind.*

Banquo was watching him. "The instruments of darkness tell us truths," Banquo said quietly, "Win us with honest trifles, to betray's / In deepest consequence."

Macbeth did not answer him. He stood with the damp coming through his boots and the three titles turning in his head, and he set them in their order: Glamis, which was his; Cawdor, which was a dying man's and would soon be his; and the king, which the sisters had promised and which Banquo had now named as if it were already his. Two truths had been told to him, and each one seemed to open a door onto a room he had not yet entered.

"Two truths are told," he said under his breath, "As happy prologues to the swelling act / Of the imperial theme."

Then he was thinking, and he could not stop. Why did he let the thought settle in him like this, why did it stir his blood so, why did he yield to that suggestion whose horrid image doth unfix my hair, and make his scalp prickle under his cap as though the wind had found it? He did not know. He only knew that the thought was there and would not leave.

Ross and Angus were already turning their horses toward Forres. Banquo gathered his reins and glanced once more at the empty moor, and then at Macbeth, and then away. Macbeth went with them, and the heath lay grey and silent behind the four riders as they went down the road.
